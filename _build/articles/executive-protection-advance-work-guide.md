When people picture executive protection, they picture the moment of arrival: a car door opening, an officer scanning the street, a principal walking briskly into a building. That moment is real, but it is the smallest part of the work. By the time the door opens, almost every meaningful decision has already been made. Those decisions are made during the advance.

Advance work is the discipline of going ahead of a principal to understand, test and prepare every place they will be and every route they will take. Done well, it turns a day full of unknowns into a day with very few surprises. Done badly, or skipped, it leaves officers improvising at the exact moment improvisation is most dangerous.

This briefing walks through what a professional advance looks like in the seventy-two hours before a principal arrives, and why each step matters.

## Why seventy-two hours

There is nothing magic about the number, but it reflects a practical reality. Seventy-two hours gives an advance team enough time to review intelligence, physically visit every location, drive every route at the right time of day, meet the people who control each venue, and still leave a full day for briefing and rehearsal. It also leaves time to change the plan if the advance reveals something the itinerary did not anticipate.

Shorter advances are possible and sometimes necessary. A twelve-hour deployment is achievable when the Operations Desk supports the field team remotely, using prior venue knowledge and trusted local partners. But compression always trades away something, usually the ability to see a route or a venue at the same hour it will be used. Lighting, traffic, crowds and staffing all change through the day, and a venue that looks controlled at ten in the morning can feel very different at nine at night.

## T–72 hours: the threat and itinerary review

The advance begins at a desk, not on a street. Analysts and the detail lead review three things together.

### The principal's exposure

Who is the principal to the outside world right now? A chief executive in the middle of a contested restructuring has a different exposure from the same executive during a quiet quarter. Recent media coverage, social media attention, litigation, activism and any history of concerning communications all shape the posture. The review also considers what is publicly knowable about the trip itself. If a conference has published the speaker schedule, the timing of the principal's arrival is effectively public.

### The itinerary

Every movement is listed with its timing, location, the people involved and the flexibility around it. The team identifies fixed points, the moments when the principal must be in a particular place at a particular time, because fixed points are where predictability concentrates risk. Where possible, the team recommends small changes that reduce that predictability: a different entrance, an earlier arrival, a car change partway through the day.

### The environment

Each city and venue is assessed for crime patterns, civil unrest, transport disruption, local law on armed protection and the quality of nearby medical care. Internationally, this includes legal considerations such as permits, licensing and what equipment can lawfully be carried.

The output of this phase is a short written assessment and a posture recommendation: how many officers, whether vehicles should be low-profile or armored, and whether the detail should be overt or quietly integrated with the principal's staff.

## T–48 hours: the physical advance

With the paper picture complete, advance agents go to the ground. This is the heart of the work, and it cannot be done from a screen.

### Routes

Every route is driven, ideally at the same hour and on the same day of the week it will be used. The agent notes choke points such as bridges, tunnels, one-way streets, construction and school zones, where a vehicle could be boxed in or forced to stop. For each primary route the team defines at least one alternate and an emergency route that leads to the nearest safe haven, which is often a hospital, a police station or a secure hotel.

Timings are recorded under realistic conditions. A route that takes eighteen minutes at midday may take forty at rush hour, and the itinerary needs to reflect the slower number.

### Venues

At each venue, the agent walks the principal's path from vehicle to room and back. They identify the drop-off point, the entrance, the route through the building, the room itself and the exits. Every venue should have a primary exit, a secondary exit and a safe room, a space with a lockable door, no exterior windows and ideally a communications signal, where the principal can be held if something goes wrong.

The agent meets the venue's security manager, facilities lead and front-of-house staff. These relationships matter more than any floor plan. A facilities manager who knows the detail lead by name will open a service corridor without hesitation when it counts.

### Medical planning

Medical readiness is non-negotiable. The advance identifies the nearest trauma center, the nearest hospital capable of cardiac care and the fastest route to each from every venue. It confirms ambulance response expectations and notes whether the principal has known medical conditions or allergies that receiving hospitals should be aware of. The detail itself carries trauma and cardiac equipment, and at least one officer is trained in advanced pre-hospital care.

> In our experience, a well-organized medical plan is invoked far more often than any other emergency plan. Most protective emergencies are medical, not violent.

## T–24 hours: briefing and rehearsal

The final day before arrival is for people, not places.

### The team brief

Every officer, driver and supporting partner is briefed together. The brief covers the principal's profile and preferences, the itinerary, the posture, the routes and alternates, the venue plans, radio and phone protocols, and the emergency action plans for medical incidents, vehicle breakdowns, protests and attacks. Each person leaves the brief knowing their position at every point in the day and what they do if the plan changes.

### Rehearsal on site

The most valuable hour of an advance is often the walk-through rehearsal. The team physically walks the principal's path at key venues, practices the vehicle drop-off and pick-up, and runs the emergency moves: evacuation to the secondary exit, movement to the safe room, and the drive to hospital. Rehearsal exposes small problems a document never will, such as a door that locks automatically after eight in the evening, or a lift that requires a key card.

### Final checks

Vehicles are inspected and fueled, equipment is checked, communications are tested between every officer and the Operations Desk, and the itinerary is reconciled one last time with the principal's office.

## Arrival: when the advance pays off

On the day, the advance turns into calm. Officers already know where they stand, where the exits are and who to call. The Operations Desk tracks every movement against the plan and flags any deviation: a road closure, a delayed flight, an unexpected protest. Because alternates have been driven and venues already have relationships in place, adjustments are quiet and quick.

The principal experiences very little of this. They see a few calm professionals and a day that runs on time. That invisibility is the point.

## After: the debrief

Every operation ends with a written debrief. What went to plan, what did not, which venue contacts were helpful, which routes were slower than expected, and what anyone noticed that felt out of place. These notes become the starting point for the next advance in the same city, which is why mature protective programs get quieter and smoother over time.

## Common advance failures

Most advance failures are not dramatic. They are small omissions that compound.

- Studying routes on a map instead of driving them at the right time of day.
- Choosing a safe room without checking that its door actually locks from the inside.
- Planning for violence but not for a medical emergency.
- Relying on a venue's floor plan instead of meeting its staff.
- Producing a beautiful advance document and never rehearsing it.
- Failing to update the plan when the itinerary changes at the last minute.

## What principals and their offices can do

The quality of an advance depends partly on the information the protective team receives. Principals and their offices can help in a few simple ways: share itineraries early and update them as they change, tell the detail about medical conditions in confidence, flag any recent concerning attention, and agree in advance how the principal prefers the detail to behave in social and business settings.

## Conclusion

Executive protection is often judged by what happens at the car door, but it is decided in the seventy-two hours before. A thorough advance gives officers knowledge instead of assumptions, relationships instead of strangers and rehearsed responses instead of improvisation. For the principal, the result is a day that feels ordinary. For the protective team, that ordinariness is the clearest sign the work was done well.
